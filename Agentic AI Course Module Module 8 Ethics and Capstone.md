# 1. Ethics in AI

### Why Ethics Comes Last

We have placed ethics at the end of the course intentionally; it is not an afterthought but the integrative lens through which all prior decisions must be evaluated. You have learned about AI strategy, governance, risk, compliance, and technology. Ethics asks the deeper questions: Just because we can apply AI, should we? Under what terms? How do we taking into account human, societal, and stakeholder implications?
This orientation connects directly to your capstone. Your final project must not only demonstrate technical acuity and strategic insight, but also **ethical judgment.** You will need to consider how your proposed AI interventions impact employees, customers, regulators, and society. Ethics, in effect, becomes the final test of responsible leadership.

### Why AI Ethics Is Different From Traditional Tech

AI differs from traditional technologies in three key ways: It is often opaque, operates probabilistically, and scales decisions rapidly, amplifying both value and risk.

Moreover, AI works at scale and speed, so errors are amplified. A flawed formula in a spreadsheet might affect one team; a flawed AI model can impact thousands or millions. This introduces new urgency for ethical foresight, making ethics not a barrier, but a strategic imperative.

### Core Ethical Principles for AI in Business

Several widely cited principles have emerged as guideposts from both practice and scholarship. Four of them stand out for leaders:

- Fairness and non-discrimination: AI can replicate or entrench bias if trained on skewed data. Business leaders must ensure systems do not disadvantage individuals based on protected characteristics. Transparency and fairness are both ethical and profitable, making AI fair can improve organizational performance (Satell & Abdel‑Magied, 2020; Kaushik & Kai, 2024).
- Transparency & Explainability: Black‑box systems breed distrust. Leaders need systems whose decisions can be meaningfully communicated to employees, customers, and regulators in plain English. 
- Accountability & Responsibility: Autonomy does not absolve responsibility. Leaders must retain oversight and ethical accountability for outcomes attributed to AI. 
- Privacy & Data Stewartship: With increasing data regulation, mismanagement is both a reputational and legal risk. Responsible leaders treat data with care and purpose. 
### Workforce Disruption and Job Loss: The Human Dimension
The single most voiced concern among employees about AI is job displacement. This is not only about lost income, but about eroded identity, purpose, and dignity. Below, we look at historical precedents, real-world behavior, and likely outcomes to get a better grasp on how the rollout of AI will affect employment.

### Business Risks of Ethical Failures
- biased hiring (Women, 2018, Amazon, AI hiring), deepfake and disinformation, healthcare misdiagnosis
Ethical AI missteps can lead to loss of trust, disengaged staff, customer backlash, and regulatory scrutiny.
In this respect, ethical failures are not just moral missteps but are business hazards. Integrating ethics into governance is not philanthropic; it is risk management.

### Practical Ethical Decision-Making Frameworks
Leaders need tools, not abstraction:
-  do not harm:  Inspired by medicine, but applied to AI interventions.
- stakeholder impact: Stakeholder impact analysis: assess how changes affect employees, customers, investors, and society.
- public transparency: Public transparency test: Would you feel comfortable explaining the decision on front‑page headlines?

### Definition: Model Card  

A model card is a standardized document that outlines an AI model’s purpose, data sources, performance, limitations, and risks. The concept, introduced by Google researchers in 2019, helps organizations evaluate whether an AI system is appropriate, fair, and safe for a given business application (Desai, 2023).

### Ethics and Corporate Leadership
Leaders set the tone. Ethical AI can become a brand differentiator when rooted in strategic clarity.
Examples: Leaders in AI ethics such as the Partnership on AI convene diverse stakeholders to set norms and guidance on responsible deployment. Leaders foster trust when they embed ethics into governance, not just in mission statements, but in operations, training, and accountability structures.
Ethical leadership balances performance with purpose. The question shifts from "How far can we push efficiency?" to "How can we advance responsibly and sustainably?" (Moltazu, 2019).

### Global and Cultural Perspectives

Ethics is not universally uniform:
- Europe often leads privacy-first norms.
- The United States favors innovation-first approaches with emerging oversight.
- Asia varies: some countries enact state-led experimentation and regulation.
Global leaders must respect legal and cultural differences while upholding core principles like fairness, accountability, and human welfare.

### Conclusion

**Ethics as the Bridge to the Capstone**

In your Capstone, you are expected to explicitly address ethical risks, trade-offs, and mitigation strategies. Key prompts include:

- What risks, especially to the workforce, does your AI strategy entail?

- How will you mitigate these risks while maintaining trust?

- How will you ensure fairness, transparency, and accountability?

Ethics is not an optional add-on; it is central to responsible leadership and strategic viability.

## 2. The Capstone

### Keep in Mind

The capstone requires the creation of a 2–5-page document outlining how AI could be integrated into a function or process within your organization. The document should address the following elements: business context, technology choice, cost considerations, security plan, change management and training, scaling strategy, governance and compliance, and key performance indicators (KPIs).

The details provided in the section below are intended to serve as a reference framework. It is understood that you may not have access to all the information needed to answer each section comprehensively. In such cases, you should specify how the missing information could be obtained, whether through internal resources, external expertise, or additional data collection.
