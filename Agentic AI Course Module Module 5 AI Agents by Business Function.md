## 1. The AI Maturity Cycle

With agentic AI, routine tasks can be transformed into strategic differentiators. As organizations harness intelligent agents, they unlock new levels of efficiency, innovation, and competitive advantage. In this section, we will provide a roadmap for moving from isolated experiments to enterprise‑wide impact, showing how to convert potential into performance.

### Understanding the AI Maturity Cycle

Inspired by insights from OpenAI’s framework for identifying and scaling AI use cases, Dr. Sanchez developed a five-stage AI maturity cycle with the aim of helping organizations to pinpoint high-value use cases, cultivate organizational readiness, and sustain AI-driven transformation.

## Stage 1: Identify Opportunities

Organizations initiate their AI journey by pinpointing tasks that are **well-suited** for **automation** or **augmentation**. Such tasks often involve repetitive processes or recurring bottlenecks.

For instance, a sales team may spend significant time conducting account research. By breaking this work into discrete actions (or workflows, as we prefer to call them), such as selecting target companies, enriching profile data, and drafting outreach messages, the team might be able to develop an AI agent that can generate a draft report within minutes rather than hours. Similarly, software engineers might employ agentic code editors to develop functional prototypes in a single workday. By requesting and refining code snippets in stages, they accelerate development cycles and reduce manual iteration.

The most important consideration at this stage is securing stakeholder buy-in. This will involve demonstrating tangible benefits and building user familiarity with AI capabilities.

## Stage 2: Teach AI Fundamentals

After identifying high-value opportunities, organizations must establish a common foundation of knowledge. In the process of cultivating AI literacy, stakeholders learn how to craft effective prompts, interpret model outputs, and anticipate typical limitations.

**Governance** Governance policies should be defined to mitigate risks such as Shadow AI, which occurs when employees use unsanctioned AI tools without authorization. Clear policies and oversight help ensure that AI use aligns with organizational standards and compliance requirements.

**Tools** To ensure consistent tool use, organizations may roll out approved large language model editors, agent platforms, and conversational analytics interfaces. Standardizing tools helps teams work efficiently while reducing fragmentation and risk.

**Ethics** Ethical guardrails guide teams by establishing standards for fairness, transparency, data privacy, and accountability. Together, these measures ensure that early AI experiments proceed safely, responsibly, and consistently.

## Stage 3: Map Workflows

Workflow mapping involves analyzing existing processes to identify where AI can be most effective. To get started, teams might reimagine their daily tasks with agentic AI support.

Dr. Sanchez uses LinkedIn account management as an illustration of workflow mapping (as seen in the diagram below) as well as a potential use case. An HR team might split the task of LinkedIn account management into distinct workflows, such as connection outreach, profile enrichment, message personalization, and engagement tracking. By mapping these components, it will help the team to envision how specialized AI agents might automate some of these tasks, thereby improving efficiency.

Once workflow maps have been generated, prototypes might be developed and scored based on effort, value, and risk. Based on these evaluations, the most promising initiatives could then be selected for further development.

## Stage 4: Provide Enablement and Resources

Once pilot programs have been selected, the organization must allocate resources to move solutions toward production readiness. Cross‑functional development teams work to integrate AI agents into secure enterprise platforms, while robust data pipelines and standardized API interfaces ensure reliable data flow. Performance dashboards are used to measure improvements in time savings, error rates, and throughput.

For example, a customer service organization might deploy an AI agent to assist with support tickets. The agent is integrated into the organization’s CRM system, connected to its knowledge bases through APIs, and supported by a data pipeline that continuously updates product information. A dashboard then tracks metrics such as average resolution time and customer satisfaction, allowing teams to evaluate performance and refine the system.  
  
During this stage, executive sponsorship and coaching are crucial to support teams through the navigation of technical and organizational challenges.
## Stage 5: Scale and Share Success

In the final stage, AI-driven processes are incorporated into standard operating procedures. Organizations publish detailed case studies that describe agent architecture, development methodology, and governance protocols. They report clear performance metrics, such as reductions in handling time and improvements in review speed.

For example, a financial services firm might report that its AI-powered document review agent reduced contract review time by 40% while maintaining compliance accuracy, enabling legal teams to process significantly higher volumes without increasing headcount.

Communities of practice and AI champions emerge to share best practices and foster continuous innovation. Embedding AI capabilities in this manner creates a virtuous cycle of adoption and organizational transformation.

### The Crawl, Walk, Run Model for AI Maturity

An alternative approach to AI adoption is the crawl, walk, run framework**.**

While the five-stage maturity cycle outlines _what_ organizations need to build, the **crawl, walk, run** model provides a practical lens for _how quickly_ and _at what level of complexity_ those capabilities should be implemented.

The crawl, walk, run model focuses on starting small with pilot projects before expanding supervised AI capabilities and ultimately scaling up to more complex and sophisticated initiatives that may involve, for instance, the implementation of fully autonomous agentic AI systems (Silver, 2025). The model enables organizations to build their AI capabilities gradually, manage risks, and generate value at each stage. (Ripla, 2025).

### The Structure of the Product Development Lifecycle
Going more deeply into the process, let us review the steps so that we can identify the points at which technological intervention can maximize efficiency and multiply productivity. While individual organizations may use slightly different labels, most product lifecycles include the following core phases:
**Ideation and Requirements**
Concept generation, stakeholder interviews, competitive research
**Design** UX/UI design, interaction flows, early system architecture﻿
**Prototyping** Building low- or high-fidelity versions of the product for internal or external feedback
**Development** Writing code, integrating systems, and preparing for launch
**Testing and QA** Validating functionality, reliability, and security
**Launch** Shipping the product to users
**Post-Launch Iteration** Monitoring, gathering feedback, making improvement

Having seen the steps involved in product development, now let us look at specific companies and how they have leveraged generative AI.

Generative AI is already being used in various parts of the PDLC to produce content, designs, structures, and data models based on patterns it has learned. It augments human creativity and speeds up tasks that once required hours of manual work. Here are four compelling examples: Tesla car design, Crocs user generated AI jeebits, Nike shoes, L'oreal formulation

### Supercharging with Agentic AI

Although we might find the previous set of examples staggering in their speed, scope, and efficiency, let us look at how that process can be taken a step further with agentic AI.

Agentic AI takes generative capabilities and adds the capacity to operate across time, act independently, pursue goals, and manage sequences of tasks. Rather than being reactive, these systems are proactive and adaptive.

## Oracle - Agentic AI for Automated Sales Communication (2025):

Oracle has developed agentic AI systems that support sales professionals by autonomously performing multi-step writing and communication tasks.
These AI agents scan customer data across platforms, analyze buyer behavior, draft personalized outreach messages, refine tone, and adapt content for different communication channels, all without human prompting at each step.
The system does not just write; it decides _what_ to write, _why_, and _when_ to send it. In this context, agentic AI plays the role of co-creator, helping scale personalized communication as a core business product (Nellis, 2025).

## IKEA – Agentic AI for Concept Exploration and Brand Reinterpretation (2023):

In a collaboration with its independent design lab SPACE10, IKEA employed agentic AI systems to explore entirely new directions in furniture design.
The AI, trained on decades of IKEA’s archival catalogs, was not used for single image generation; it functioned as a creative collaborator, autonomously producing and refining hundreds of design concepts across aesthetic themes, decades, and functional constraints.
Designers could then interact with these outputs, guiding the system to explore new branches of style, form, or era.
This iterative, responsive process positions the AI not just as a tool, but as a partner in navigating IKEA’s design heritage and future possibilities (Fast Company, 2023).

In these examples, agentic AI is not just helping produce outputs. It is actively shaping processes, iterating, testing, and deciding. It acts not as a tool, but as a collaborator.

We have seen a stunning array of ways in which both generative and agentic AI are used in the design process. Although not all companies produce physical wares, keep in mind that in the digital economy, information itself is a product (see the example of Oracle above).

## 3. AI Agents by Business Function Across the Enterprise

As organizations progress through their AI maturity journey, they discover that various business functions require specialized AI agent capabilities. These are not merely chatbots or basic automation tools. They are sophisticated systems that perceive context, make decisions, and take actions independently to accomplish complex business objectives.

### Understanding Functional AI Agent Deployments
Functional AI agent deployments are the practical applications of agentic AI within specific business domains. These agents are purpose-built to understand the unique workflows, regulations, and requirements of their respective functions, unlike general-purpose AI tools. They use specialized knowledge bases, integrate with function-specific systems, and comply with industry standards.

### Functional Use Cases

### HR Bots: Transforming Human Resource Operations

HR bots are among the most mature applications of agentic AI in enterprise environments. These systems perform more than basic question-answering. They independently manage complex, multi-step human resources processes.

- Recruitment and Talent Acquisition
 - Employee Onboarding and Support
- Benefits Administration and Policy Management
### Financial Advisors: Intelligent Wealth Management

Financial advisor AI agents transform wealth management by automating complex tasks such as client onboarding, compliance, and portfolio analysis. These systems analyze large volumes of financial data in real time. They identify trends and deliver personalized investment advice. These agents streamline routine tasks. They enable advisors to focus on strategic client engagement and informed decision-making.

-  Advanced Analytics and Decision Support
- Client Onboarding and KYC Processing
- Portfolio Management and Risk Assessment
- Predictive Analytics and Market Intelligence

### Enterprise-Grade Financial Solutions

Enterprise-grade financial solutions leverage advanced AI agents designed to meet strict regulatory and compliance requirements while delivering personalized, real-time financial insights. These solutions enable financial institutions to enhance decision-making and streamline complex processes securely.

### IT Copilots: Autonomous Technical Support

IT copilots are advanced AI systems that automate various technical support tasks. These range from ticket management to system monitoring and troubleshooting. These agents independently handle multi-step workflows. They reduce manual workload and accelerate issue resolution. They provide proactive alerts and self-service options. These improve IT efficiency and user satisfaction.

- Advanced Problem Resolution
- Intelligent Help Desk Operations
- Proactive System Monitoring
- Self-Service and Automation

## 4. Agentic AI in Health Care: Key Applications and Impact

Health care is being transformed by agentic AI systems, which are intelligent agents that autonomously perform complex tasks. These agents now assist doctors and hospitals in many ways, from diagnosing patients to managing hospital logistics. For example, in controlled benchmark scenarios, Microsoft’s AI orchestrator correctly diagnosed 85.5% of challenging medical cases (compared with about 20% by doctors) and did so at a lower cost (King et al., n.d.). Industry experts agree these AI agents will revolutionize patient care and operations. Below, we examine the main application areas and their effects on patients and hospital efficiency.

### Clinical Decision Support (Diagnosis and Treatment)

Agentic AI agents can analyze patient data and iteratively reason like a doctor. They review symptoms, lab results, and histories, then ask follow-up questions or order tests automatically until they reach a diagnosis. This sequential decision-making improves accuracy and speed. For example, Microsoft’s AI Diagnostic Orchestrator (MAI-DxO) emulates a panel of physicians. It reached 85% accuracy on a complex case challenge, more than four times better than physicians, while using fewer resources. Similarly, Duke University’s Sepsis Watch agent continuously monitors hundreds of vital-sign variables and can detect sepsis hours before it becomes clinically obvious (King et al., n.d.).

**Positive impact on patient care and Operations**
### Medical Imaging and Radiology

Agentic AI is also automating image analysis. Intelligent imaging agents scan X-rays, MRIs, CTs, and pathology slides nonstop, flagging urgent findings for human review. These agents work continuously without fatigue and apply consistent vigilance to every image.

For example, Aidoc is an AI assistant for radiology. It automatically analyzes every emergency CT scan and instantly alerts radiologists to life-threatening conditions such as brain bleeds or clots, dramatically reducing report turnaround times. Another platform, RadNet, pre-screens mammograms so that only suspicious cases reach the radiologist, improving cancer detection while reducing routine load (Simbie, 2025).
### Personalized Care and Coordination

Agentic AI helps deliver tailored treatments and smooth care coordination. Agents can analyze a patient’s genetics, history, and test results to recommend the most effective therapies.

For example, oncology agents such as Watson for Oncology examine medical literature and clinical trial databases to suggest cancer treatments matched to a patient’s tumor profile. In practice, multidisciplinary teams are using AI “virtual tumor boards.” Agents aggregate imaging, pathology, and history, summarize key points, and highlight relevant trials so that doctors can plan treatment more efficiently (Microsoft, 2025).

Agents also automate care logistics. In one pilot, an AI coordinator automatically scheduled a patient’s urgent MRI, while another agent checked the patient’s implanted pacemaker model to ensure the scan would be safe. The orchestrator then alerted the oncologist with a draft treatment plan (Kass-Hout et al., 2024). Together, these examples show how agentic AI coordinates both treatment decisions and the logistics required to deliver them.
### Operations and Administration

Beyond direct care, agentic AI is streamlining hospital operations. Agents manage scheduling, staffing, and logistics with real-time data.

For example, Epic’s Bed Management agent continuously processes admissions and discharges and autonomously assigns the most appropriate bed for each incoming patient, taking into account care team location and infection control (Simbie, 2025). This reduces emergency room wait times. Other AI agents manage appointment calendars, predict patient admission surges, or reorder supplies when needed.

## 5. Agentic AI in Browsers: Potentials and Risks

### Why Browsers Matter in Business Functions

For most knowledge workers, the browser is not merely a tool; it constitutes the digital home of their workday. Whether employees are managing email, editing documents, collaborating on projects, reviewing dashboards, or accessing HR systems, the majority of such activity occurs within a browser. Research indicates that many users maintain six to ten tabs open simultaneously, underscoring the browser’s central role in organizational workflows (Verma, 2025).

Traditionally, browsers functioned as transparent windows to the internet, essentially passive conduits. This role, however, is changing. A new generation of browsers has been described as a shift from static interfaces toward active AI environments, thereby blurring the line between browsing and working (Shoukat, 2025; Caswell, 2025). In this sense, the browser is evolving into a platform for agency, one that not only presents information but also assists in its interpretation, summarization, and practical application.
### How AI Is Transforming Browsers

A useful comparison highlights just how radical this transformation is: earlier browsers functioned like a 1950s card catalog; they were static, manual, and confined. In contrast, today’s AI enhanced browsers operate more like a personal assistant, who is well-read, analytical, and capable of producing polished writing instantly. Those who adapt quickly to this evolution, gain a clear competitive advantage.

### Business Use Cases Across Functions
**Human Resources** teams spend much of their time in browser-based tools such as applicant tracking systems and LinkedIn. AI-enabled browsers can streamline résumé review by summarizing candidate profiles or generating side-by-side comparisons based on chosen criteria. As Vonage notes, HR leaders are increasingly turning to AI to support recruitment processes, from résumé screening to scheduling, thereby freeing professionals to focus on higher-value activities such as employee engagement and retention (Vonage, 2024). In practice, the browser becomes a central assistant for speeding up repetitive HR tasks while preserving human judgment for final decisions.

Browser AI can also assist **finance teams** by summarizing regulatory filings, competitor financial reports, or internal PDF documents, all within the browser itself. This enables analysts to digest dense information more rapidly and identify key trends without switching between multiple tools. The convenience and speed of this approach often translate directly into faster, data-driven decision-making.

**Marketing** professionals benefit from browser AI when drafting copy, analyzing market trends, or translating content. Rather than bouncing between ChatGPT and browser tabs, these AI functions are often embedded directly into marketing dashboards or editorial platforms, allowing for real-time ideation and execution in one seamless environment.

Technical specialists are beginning to benefit from browser-embedded AI tools that assist directly within development consoles. For example, Google’s Chrome DevTools now includes a feature with Gemini AI that allows developers to hover over error messages in the Console and click an “Understand this error” button. The AI then explains the issue and suggests possible corrections, all without copying and pasting error codes (Emmanuel, 2025). While not a replacement for deep technical expertise, this feature offers a faster, contextual first step for diagnosing and addressing errors.

**Cross-Functional Impact:**  
Perhaps the most transformative advantage is the consistency across functions. Whether preparing for a meeting, researching a firm, or drafting an email, AI-enabled browsers speed up workflows for all staff. This uniform experience reduces cognitive switching costs and elevates organizational productivity.

### Risks and Limitations

AI-enabled browsers open exciting possibilities, but they also introduce serious risks that leaders must manage carefully. Data leaks, misinformation, and overreliance can all undermine trust, damage reputations, and expose organizations to legal or regulatory consequences. These challenges remind us that while the technology is powerful, it is not foolproof. To use AI responsibly, businesses must pair adoption with strong guardrails, training, and oversight.

## Overreliance

Convenience can tempt employees to accept AI-generated summaries or suggestions in the browser without verifying them. That is risky because large language models are prone to confident errors. The now famous example of the lawyer, Steven Schwart, who used Chat GPT to prepare his case against Colombia-based Avianca Airlines, should be a lesson to us all. Unbeknownst to Schwarts, his “research” included, “bogus judicial decisions with bogus quotes and bogus internal citations.” In short, it was fiction: convincing sounding gibberish.

More recent misfires demonstrate that we are still in the era of hallucinations: Google’s AI Overviews has answered basic prompts incorrectly, including asserting the wrong year (Wired, 2025).

## 6. Agent Architecture in the Enterprise: Centralized vs. Embedded

### The Issue of Central and Embedded

Although the title of this unit may sound highly technical and abstract, the central ideas are common to other realms. Imagine a global clothing brand known for high‑quality, handmade garments. Let’s consider two possible versions of this store.

- a **centralized system**, where a single general‑purpose assistant supports the enterprise from a core location
- an **embedded model**, where specialized agents live inside departmental tools and workflows.
### The Centralized Agent Model

In a centralized model, an organization typically deploys one or two powerful agents that serve as general‑purpose assistants across departments. These agents often operate through familiar platforms like Slack or Microsoft Teams and are supported by an orchestration layer that routes user queries to the right systems.

Centralized agents are most useful when an organization is just beginning to explore the use of generative or agentic AI. They create a common interface for employees, simplify governance, and help leadership understand where demand and friction points exist across different departments.

#### example centralized in Slack AskNoa
Valeria works in operations at a mid‑sized logistics company. Every morning, she opens Slack and starts her day with a few quick questions to the company’s agent, AskNova. She asks, “How many PTO days do I have left?” and gets an instant reply based on her HR record. Later, she requests a summary of a 27‑page vendor contract. AskNova extracts the key terms, flags unusual clauses, and provides a plain‑language summary she can forward to her manager.

Valeria never leaves Slack. She does not need to log into HR software, open a legal folder, or ping someone in legal operations. Behind the scenes, AskNova uses a single LLM‑based orchestration layer that queries various systems through APIs. Valeria does not see the complexity, but she benefits from it.

This centralized setup creates a smooth, consistent user experience and significantly reduces first‑line requests to HR, IT, and legal departments. It also allows the AI team to monitor queries and improve the assistant over time using one shared pipeline.

### The Embedded Agent Model
As organizations mature in their use of AI, some discover the limits of centralization. While general‑purpose agents can handle many types of requests, they often fall short in specialized domains. Departments such as finance, marketing, and design may need agents trained on unique data, tools, and workflows.

In the embedded model, smaller agents are placed directly inside the systems teams already use. A finance agent might live in Excel or a planning platform. A design assistant might operate within Figma or Notion. These agents are context‑aware, tailored to the specific needs of the function, and can execute more complex or high‑stakes tasks.

#### example marketing
When the centralized assistant was first deployed at a consumer goods company, it proved useful for answering HR questions and summarizing meeting transcripts. But the marketing team quickly reached its limits with it. Their work required creative ideation, brand nuance, and campaign‑specific knowledge. The centralized agent offered bland suggestions, ignored the company’s voice, and occasionally reused outdated slogans.

Eventually, the company spun up a specialized marketing agent trained on historical campaigns, brand guidelines, and customer personas. It was embedded into their project management tool, where it could generate drafts, compare tone variants, and even suggest social copy based on recent product launches. Unlike the general agent, this embedded assistant understood context and offered content ready for immediate use.

This shift marked the beginning of a more federated, embedded approach across departments.

### Security, Compliance, and Governance

Security and compliance are also important considerations when choosing an agent architecture. Centralized agents are generally easier to manage because controls such as access permissions, logging, and audit trails can be handled in one place. This makes it simpler to meet standards such as HIPAA and other data protection regulations.

Embedded agents, on the other hand, often operate across different tools, data sets, and teams. Because they are distributed, they can introduce risks if not managed carefully—for example, through inconsistent permissions, data exposure, or gaps in oversight.

To address this, many organizations adopt a hybrid approach with shared governance. This allows teams to build specialized solutions while maintaining consistent standards for security, compliance, and accountability across the enterprise (Satish, 2025).

Consider a U.S.‑based health care network exploring agent deployment across departments. A centralized agent that handles general scheduling queries or policy FAQs can be contained within strict access controls and monitored for compliance with HIPAA requirements. But when a specialized agent is embedded directly into a patient intake system or billing platform, the risk exposure increases. If the embedded agent generates, stores, or even temporarily processes protected health information (PHI) without proper safeguards—such as encryption, access logs, and business associate agreements—the organization could face regulatory penalties or legal liability. In this case, centralizing sensitive data access through a governed layer may not only improve efficiency, but also reduce compliance risk.

### A Hybrid Path: Centralized Core with Embedded Specialists

Most organizations do not stay in one model forever. Instead, they evolve toward a hybrid approach. A centralized agent remains in place as a gateway or “generalist,” handling common tasks and routing more complex queries to embedded specialists. Departments that reach a certain level of maturity or scale develop their own agents tailored to their needs.

This mirrors the clothing brand analogy. The core product line continues to come from central sourcing, ensuring consistency and brand recognition. But regional shops gain permission to offer locally sourced garments that reflect the needs of their customers. The result is a flexible architecture that combines the strengths of both models.

### Maturity Phases and Architectural Shifts

Agent architecture is rarely static. It evolves alongside organizational needs and internal AI fluency. Referring back to the maturity model discussed in previous sections, the following steps help map architectural decisions to readiness levels:

## Crawl: Centralized Only

- One assistant serves the whole organization
- Focus on simple Q&A, basic document summarization, policy lookup
- Central control and monitoring

## Walk: Central With Early Embedded Experiments

- Central agent remains dominant, but early embedded pilots begin (e.g., HR, customer support)
- Specialized teams start building or requesting context‑specific agents
- Knowledge of prompt engineering and retrieval grows inside departments

## Run: Federated and Modular

- Multiple embedded agents operate within departments
- Shared infrastructure or orchestration layer ensures interoperability
- Local ownership of training, fine‑tuning, and update cycles
- Central team governs security, performance, and compliance standards

### Conclusion

The choice between centralized and embedded agent architecture should not be treated as binary or fixed. It is often dictated by organizational maturity, functional demand, and tolerance for complexity. While centralization offers clarity and control, embedded agents offer precision and performance. Both are valid—what matters is how clearly the design matches the organization’s goals and readiness.  
As more enterprises move beyond pilots and into production‑scale deployments, understanding this architectural spectrum will be essential. The right structure will determine not only how well the agents perform, but how well people trust, adopt, and depend on them in daily work.

## 7. The Role of Consultants in Transformation Journeys

### AI Disruption in Business Process Outsourcing (BPO)

Business process outsourcing (BPO) refers to the practice of delegating non-core business functions, such as customer support, HR, finance, accounting, research, and knowledge management, to specialized service providers. This model allows large enterprises (e.g., Accenture, Infosys, Tata, Cognizant, Wipro) to manage operations more efficiently by offloading tasks that are not central to their core competencies.

Originating in the 1940s with manufacturing support services, BPO has evolved into a $300+ billion global industries, projected to exceed $500 billion by 2030. (Tan, 2025). It now touches nearly every major sector, including retail, logistics, telecom, healthcare, banking, and insurance.

### Why Legacy Software Could Not Solve It?

Traditional software performs well when handling clearly defined, repetitive tasks with minimal variation and structured data. However, many BPO activities, such as handling customer queries or processing invoices, demand the ability to interpret unstructured information, understand context, and make judgment-based decisions. Lacking these capabilities, conventional software was inadequate. This is where modern AI excels: it can do the following:

- Process unstructured data
- Connect with multiple systems
- Carry out actions or provide recommendations effectively
